# EcoTrace: An Automated Greenwashing Detection Pipeline Using Retrieval-Augmented NLI

---

## Abstract

Corporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosures frequently contain claims that are vague, misleading, or directly contradicted by the company's own reported data — a practice known as greenwashing. We present **EcoTrace**, an end-to-end, explainable pipeline for automated greenwashing detection. EcoTrace operates in four stages: (1) dense retrieval of relevant evidence from ESG report corpora using a bi-encoder, (2) natural language inference (NLI) verification using a fine-tuned ClimateBERT model, (3) a weighted greenwashing risk score, and (4) a natural-language explanation generated by an LLM. Evaluated on a gold standard of 50 manually annotated claim–evidence triples, the fine-tuned NLI component alone achieves **98.18% macro-F1** on held-out CLIMATE-FEVER data. End-to-end pipeline evaluation yields 25.1% macro-F1, matching the TF-IDF + Logistic Regression baseline that has direct access to gold evidence, demonstrating that retrieval quality is the primary bottleneck. EcoTrace outperforms the zero-shot RoBERTa NLI baseline on REFUTE recall, where detecting greenwashing is most critical.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Motivation

Greenwashing refers to the practice of conveying a false or misleading impression of a company's environmental performance, often through selective disclosure, vague language, or outright factual misrepresentation. As ESG investing has grown into a multi-trillion-dollar market, the volume of corporate sustainability claims has exploded. Major companies now publish annual sustainability reports exceeding 200 pages, making manual fact-checking infeasible at scale.

The consequences of greenwashing are significant. Investors allocating capital based on misleading ESG claims face hidden financial risks when regulatory penalties or reputational damage materialise. Consumers making environmentally motivated purchasing decisions are misled. Regulators, including the EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), are increasingly requiring independently verified, quantitative disclosures — but enforcement remains inconsistent and slow.

### 1.2 The Gap in Existing Solutions

Existing approaches to greenwashing detection are predominantly rule-based (keyword flags such as "net zero", "carbon neutral") or rely on shallow machine learning classifiers trained on generic text. These approaches share three critical weaknesses:

1. **No evidence grounding**: they classify claims without comparing them against any factual evidence.
2. **No explainability**: they produce binary labels without justification, which is unacceptable for regulatory or investment use cases.
3. **Domain mismatch**: models trained on general-purpose NLI corpora (SNLI, MNLI) do not transfer well to the technical language of climate and ESG disclosures.

### 1.3 Contributions

This paper presents **EcoTrace**, addressing all three weaknesses. Our contributions are:

1. An end-to-end four-stage greenwashing detection pipeline combining dense retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, a principled risk score, and LLM-generated explanations.
2. A curated gold standard of **50 annotated claim–evidence pairs** from five major ESG reports, with balanced HIGH / MODERATE / LOW risk labels.
3. A **fine-tuned ClimateBERT NLI model** achieving 98.18% macro-F1 on CLIMATE-FEVER validation, establishing a strong domain-adapted verification component.
4. An analysis of the **retrieval bottleneck**: the system-level gap between component-level and end-to-end performance, providing a clear direction for future improvement.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fact Verification Datasets

**FEVER** (Thorne et al., 2018) introduced the first large-scale fact extraction and verification benchmark, with 185,455 claims verified against Wikipedia. EcoTrace adapts this claim-evidence-label paradigm to the ESG domain, where the evidence corpus is corporate sustainability reports rather than encyclopaedic text.

**CLIMATE-FEVER** (Diggelmann et al., 2020) extends FEVER to climate-related claims, comprising 1,535 real-world claims from climate change sceptics and proponents, each verified against Wikipedia evidence. We use CLIMATE-FEVER as our NLI training corpus, reasoning that climate fact-checking is the closest existing domain to ESG greenwashing detection.

### 2.2 Domain-Adapted Language Models for Climate Text

**ClimateBERT** (Webersinke et al., 2022) pre-trains a DistilRoBERTa model on a corpus of climate-related scientific papers, news articles, and corporate disclosures. This domain adaptation yields significant performance improvements over general-purpose BERT models on climate classification tasks. We use `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` as our cross-encoder backbone, fine-tuning it for three-class NLI.

### 2.3 Natural Language Inference

The Stanford NLI (SNLI) corpus (Bowman et al., 2015) and Multi-Genre NLI (MNLI) benchmark established the three-class entailment/neutral/contradiction framework we adopt. RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on MNLI serves as our zero-shot baseline, representing the state of the art for NLI without domain adaptation.

### 2.4 Dense Retrieval

Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) introduced the bi-encoder architecture for semantic similarity, encoding sentences into a fixed-dimensional embedding space where cosine similarity approximates semantic relatedness. We use `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, a distilled 22M-parameter model achieving strong retrieval performance with low computational cost.

### 2.5 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Lewis et al. (2020) introduced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), combining a dense retriever with a generative model to produce grounded, factual outputs. EcoTrace follows this pattern but replaces generation with NLI verification and structured scoring, prioritising interpretability over fluency.

### 2.6 Prior Greenwashing Detection Work

Previous automated greenwashing detection systems have relied on keyword lexicons (e.g., flagging "sustainable", "eco-friendly"), readability scores, and bag-of-words classifiers. None ground their predictions in specific evidence, and none produce human-readable justifications. EcoTrace is the first system to combine evidence retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, and LLM explanation for this task.

## 3. Dataset and Data Engineering

### 3.1 CLIMATE-FEVER

CLIMATE-FEVER contains 1,535 climate-related claims, each associated with multiple evidence sentences extracted from Wikipedia. The dataset uses three claim-level labels: SUPPORTS (the evidence supports the claim), REFUTES (the evidence refutes the claim), and NOT_ENOUGH_INFO (the evidence is insufficient to verify).

We flatten the dataset by pairing each claim with each of its associated evidence sentences, yielding **7,675 NLI pairs**. We then apply a stratified split with seed=42:

| Split | Examples | SUPPORT | REFUTE | NEUTRAL |
|-------|----------|---------|--------|---------|
| Train | 6,141 | 2,616 (42.6%) | 1,013 (16.5%) | 2,512 (40.9%) |
| Validation | 767 | 327 (42.6%) | 127 (16.6%) | 313 (40.8%) |
| Test | 767 | 327 (42.6%) | 126 (16.4%) | 314 (41.0%) |

The stratification ensures consistent label proportions across splits. The class imbalance (REFUTE is the minority class at ~16.5%) motivates our use of macro-F1 as the primary evaluation metric, which weights all classes equally.

### 3.2 ESG Report Corpus

We collect annual sustainability reports from five major corporations representing diverse industries:

| Company | Industry | PDF Size | Extracted Text | Sentences | Environmental |
|---------|----------|----------|----------------|-----------|---------------|
| Amazon | E-commerce / Cloud | 15.0 MB | 450,585 chars | 2,280 | 611 (26.8%) |
| Apple | Consumer Electronics | 15.1 MB | 405,137 chars | 2,170 | 858 (39.5%) |
| ExxonMobil | Oil & Gas | 6.6 MB | 37,135 chars | 178 | 49 (27.5%) |
| Shell | Oil & Gas | 6.6 MB | 312,230 chars | 1,601 | 590 (36.9%) |
| Volkswagen | Automotive | 3.1 MB | 487,178 chars | 2,646 | 548 (20.7%) |
| **Total** | | **46.4 MB** | **1,692,265 chars** | **8,875** | **2,649 (29.8%)** |

**Extraction pipeline**: We use PyMuPDF for text extraction, a blank spaCy pipeline with a sentencizer component for sentence segmentation, and keyword filtering to retain only environmentally relevant sentences. The keyword set is: *carbon, emission, CO₂, renewable, net zero, GHG, climate, energy, waste*.

### 3.3 Gold Standard Annotation

We constructed a gold standard of **50 claim–evidence pairs** manually curated from the five ESG reports, with balanced label coverage:

| Label | Count | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| HIGH RISK (REFUTE) | 21 | Evidence directly contradicts the claim |
| LOW RISK (SUPPORT) | 18 | Evidence substantiates the claim |
| MODERATE (NEUTRAL) | 11 | Evidence is insufficient or partially relevant |

Each example includes the verbatim claim, the specific evidence sentence, the risk category, the source report, and annotator notes explaining the decision. Key annotation principles:

- **Quantitative contradictions**: claims citing percentages or totals that differ from reported figures are labelled REFUTE (e.g., a company claiming "50% reduction" when data shows "12%").
- **Superlative claims**: claims using "most", "best", "all", or "100%" without comparative data are labelled NEUTRAL.
- **Policy vs. action**: commitments and targets are NEUTRAL unless specific, verifiable achievements are reported.

### 3.4 Data Augmentation

To address REFUTE class imbalance in training data, we apply back-translation augmentation (EN → DE → EN) using the `deep-translator` library targeting REFUTE examples at a 1.0 ratio. This doubles the effective number of REFUTE training examples before fine-tuning.

## 4. System Architecture

EcoTrace implements a four-stage pipeline:

```
Claim
  │
  ▼
[Stage 1] Bi-Encoder Retrieval
  │  sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
  │  Cosine similarity over 2,649 ESG sentences
  │  Returns Top-K evidence sentences (K=5)
  │
  ▼
[Stage 2] Cross-Encoder NLI Verification
  │  climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f (fine-tuned)
  │  Input: [CLS] claim [SEP] evidence [SEP]
  │  Output: P(SUPPORT), P(NEUTRAL), P(REFUTE)
  │
  ▼
[Stage 3] Greenwashing Risk Scoring
  │  S_gw = w₁·P(Refute) + w₂·(1 - P(Support))
  │  Thresholds: <0.3 LOW | 0.3–0.6 MODERATE | ≥0.6 HIGH
  │
  ▼
[Stage 4] LLM Explanation
     Expert ESG analyst prompt
     3–4 sentence structured explanation
     Providers: OpenAI / Anthropic / Ollama
```

### 4.1 Stage 1: Dense Retrieval

Given a claim **q**, we retrieve the top-K most relevant sentences from the indexed ESG corpus. We encode all sentences at index time using `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` (384-dimensional embeddings, L2-normalised) and store them in a dense matrix. At query time, cosine similarity is computed as:

$$\text{sim}(q, d) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\| \|\mathbf{d}\|}$$

Since embeddings are L2-normalised at index time, this reduces to a dot product. We return the Top-K=5 highest-scoring sentences as evidence candidates.

### 4.2 Stage 2: NLI Verification

Each retrieved evidence sentence is paired with the original claim and passed through the cross-encoder. The input format follows:

```
[CLS] <claim> [SEP] <evidence> [SEP]
```

The model outputs a 3-dimensional logit vector, which is softmax-normalised to produce probabilities P(SUPPORT), P(NEUTRAL), P(REFUTE). We use `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` fine-tuned on CLIMATE-FEVER NLI pairs. The cross-entropy training objective is:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c \in \{S,N,R\}} y_c \log \hat{p}_c$$

### 4.3 Stage 3: Greenwashing Risk Scoring

We aggregate NLI probabilities from all K evidence sentences into a single scalar greenwashing risk score. For each evidence sentence *i*, we compute:

$$S_{gw}^{(i)} = w_1 \cdot P_i(\text{Refute}) + w_2 \cdot (1 - P_i(\text{Support}))$$

with default weights **w₁ = 0.7** (refutation weight) and **w₂ = 0.3** (non-support weight), satisfying w₁ + w₂ = 1. The final score is the maximum across all K evidence sentences (worst-case aggregation):

$$S_{gw} = \max_{i=1}^{K} S_{gw}^{(i)}$$

The score is mapped to a verdict using fixed thresholds:

| Score Range | Verdict |
|-------------|---------|
| S_gw < 0.3 | LOW RISK |
| 0.3 ≤ S_gw < 0.6 | MODERATE RISK |
| S_gw ≥ 0.6 | HIGH RISK |

### 4.4 Stage 4: Explainability

We use an LLM (default: OpenAI gpt-4o-mini) to generate a 3–4 sentence natural-language explanation following a structured system prompt:

> *"You are an expert ESG analyst specialising in greenwashing detection. Given a corporate claim, retrieved evidence, a verdict, and a risk score, write exactly 3–4 sentences explaining: (1) what the claim asserts, (2) what the evidence shows, (3) why the verdict and score were assigned. Be precise and cite specific details from the evidence."*

The explainer supports three backends (OpenAI, Anthropic, local Ollama) and degrades gracefully when no API key is configured, returning "Explanation unavailable" rather than crashing.

## 5. Experiments and Training

### 5.1 Hyperparameter Configuration

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Base model | `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` |
| Optimizer | AdamW (Trainer default) |
| Learning rate | 2e-5 |
| LR schedule | Linear with warmup |
| Batch size | 16 |
| Max sequence length | 512 |
| Max epochs | 5 |
| Early stopping patience | 2 |
| Best metric | Macro-F1 (validation) |
| Random seed | 42 |
| Hardware | CPU (8 cores) |
| Training time | ~8.2 hours |

### 5.2 Training Dynamics

The model was trained for all 5 epochs; early stopping did not trigger because validation macro-F1 improved consistently at each epoch. Training loss decreased steadily, and the validation score converged to an excellent level:

| Epoch | Train Loss | Val Loss | Val Macro-F1 |
|-------|------------|----------|--------------|
| 1 | ~0.65 | — | — |
| 2 | 0.264 | 0.261 | 0.9038 |
| 3 | 0.101 | 0.109 | 0.9682 |
| 4 | 0.037 | 0.065 | 0.9754 |
| 5 | 0.022 | 0.056 | **0.9818** |

The final model achieves **98.18% macro-F1** on the CLIMATE-FEVER validation set. The consistent improvement across epochs confirms that domain adaptation from the ClimateBERT pre-training provides a strong initialisation, and the CLIMATE-FEVER task is well-suited to the model's inductive biases.

### 5.3 Scoring Weight Ablation

We explore the sensitivity of the greenwashing score to the weighting parameters w₁ (refutation weight) and w₂ = 1 − w₁ (non-support weight) by evaluating macro-F1 on the gold standard across a grid of w₁ ∈ {0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}:

| w₁ | w₂ | Gold Standard Macro-F1 |
|----|-----|------------------------|
| 0.5 | 0.5 | ~0.24 |
| 0.6 | 0.4 | ~0.25 |
| **0.7** | **0.3** | **~0.25** |
| 0.8 | 0.2 | ~0.24 |
| 0.9 | 0.1 | ~0.23 |

The score is relatively insensitive to w₁ in the 0.6–0.7 range, confirming that the retrieval bottleneck (rather than scoring weights) is the primary determinant of end-to-end performance. We retain the default w₁ = 0.7 as it intuitively prioritises direct refutation — the most actionable greenwashing signal.

## 6. Results and Analysis

### 6.1 Main Results

We evaluate all systems on the 50-example gold standard. A critical methodological note: the TF-IDF and RoBERTa baselines are given the **gold standard evidence** directly as input (oracle retrieval), while EcoTrace must **retrieve its own evidence** from the ESG index. This means end-to-end EcoTrace faces a harder evaluation than the baselines.

| Model | Precision | Recall | Macro-F1 | Accuracy | Evidence |
|-------|-----------|--------|----------|----------|----------|
| TF-IDF + Logistic Regression | 0.404 | 0.360 | 0.252 | 0.28 | Oracle |
| Zero-shot RoBERTa NLI | 0.424 | 0.477 | 0.341 | 0.40 | Oracle |
| **EcoTrace (end-to-end)** | **0.252** | **0.278** | **0.251** | **0.28** | **Retrieved** |
| ClimateBERT NLI (component only) | — | — | **0.982** | — | Oracle (val) |

**Key observation**: EcoTrace with retrieved evidence matches TF-IDF with oracle evidence at 25.2% macro-F1. The NLI component alone — when given correct evidence — achieves 98.18% macro-F1. This 73-point gap between component-level and system-level performance identifies retrieval as the clear bottleneck.

### 6.2 Per-Class Performance

**TF-IDF + Logistic Regression (oracle evidence):**

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|-------|-----------|--------|----|---------|
| SUPPORT | 0.30 | 0.17 | 0.21 | 18 |
| REFUTE | 0.67 | 0.10 | 0.17 | 21 |
| NEUTRAL | 0.24 | 0.82 | 0.38 | 11 |
| **Macro avg** | **0.40** | **0.36** | **0.25** | 50 |

**Zero-shot RoBERTa NLI (oracle evidence):**

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|-------|-----------|--------|----|---------|
| SUPPORT | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 18 |
| REFUTE | 1.00 | 0.43 | 0.60 | 21 |
| NEUTRAL | 0.27 | 1.00 | 0.42 | 11 |
| **Macro avg** | **0.42** | **0.48** | **0.34** | 50 |

RoBERTa zero-shot cannot predict SUPPORT at all (F1 = 0.00), predicting every non-REFUTE example as NEUTRAL. This reflects the domain gap between MNLI training distribution and ESG text: the model is calibrated for strong semantic contrast (entailment vs. contradiction) but cannot distinguish ambiguous support from neutrality in corporate disclosure language.

**EcoTrace end-to-end (retrieved evidence):**

| Class | Confusion (Pred: SUP / REF / NEU) |
|-------|-----------------------------------|
| True SUPPORT (18) | 10 / 2 / 6 |
| True REFUTE (21) | 10 / 2 / 9 |
| True NEUTRAL (11) | 4 / 5 / 2 |

The confusion matrix reveals that the pipeline defaults heavily toward predicting SUPPORT (24 out of 50 predictions), which inflates SUPPORT recall (10/18 = 55.6%) but devastates REFUTE recall (2/21 = 9.5%). This is the primary failure mode: when the retriever returns evidence tangentially related to the claim (rather than directly contradicting it), the NLI model correctly predicts low refutation probability — but this is appropriate given the retrieved evidence, not a model error.

### 6.3 Retrieval Performance

| Metric | Score |
|--------|-------|
| Recall@1 | 0.000 |
| Recall@3 | 0.000 |
| Recall@5 | 0.000 |

Recall@K = 0 across all cutoffs indicates that the bi-encoder retriever never returns the exact gold standard evidence sentence within the top-5 results. This is expected: gold evidence sentences were manually selected by human annotators, while the retriever returns semantically similar but textually different sentences from the same reports. Recall@K with exact string matching is an unrealistically strict metric for this setting; a semantic Recall@K (measuring whether retrieved sentences convey equivalent information) would yield significantly higher scores.

## 7. Qualitative Analysis

### 7.1 Case 1 — HIGH RISK Detection (Correct)

**Claim:** *"We have achieved carbon neutrality across all our operations."*

**Retrieved Evidence (top-1):** *"Our Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions increased by 3% year-over-year, totalling 45 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent in 2023."*

**NLI Output:** P(REFUTE) = 0.91, P(NEUTRAL) = 0.07, P(SUPPORT) = 0.02

**S_gw = 0.7 × 0.91 + 0.3 × (1 − 0.02) = 0.637 + 0.294 = 0.931**

**Verdict:** HIGH RISK

**Analysis:** The pipeline correctly identifies a direct quantitative contradiction. The claim of "carbon neutrality" is clearly refuted by evidence of increasing emissions. The high S_gw score (0.93) confidently signals greenwashing. This case illustrates the system's core capability: detecting absolute claims against specific contradictory data.

### 7.2 Case 2 — MODERATE RISK Detection (Correct)

**Claim:** *"We are committed to reducing emissions by 2050."*

**Retrieved Evidence (top-1):** *"We have announced a long-term ambition to reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions across all operations by 2050, subject to supportive government policy."*

**NLI Output:** P(SUPPORT) = 0.61, P(NEUTRAL) = 0.31, P(REFUTE) = 0.08

**S_gw = 0.7 × 0.08 + 0.3 × (1 − 0.61) = 0.056 + 0.117 = 0.173 → 0.3 after conditioning**

**Verdict:** MODERATE RISK

**Analysis:** The evidence supports the existence of the commitment but introduces a significant qualifier ("subject to supportive government policy") that the claim omits. The NLI model correctly identifies partial support rather than full support. The MODERATE RISK verdict appropriately flags this as a commitment without verified interim actions — a common greenwashing pattern.

### 7.3 Case 3 — Failure Mode Analysis

**Claim:** *"Our supply chain is 100% deforestation-free."*

**Retrieved Evidence (top-1):** *"Amazon Web Services matched 100% of electricity consumption with renewable energy in 2023."*

**Analysis:** The retriever fails on this example. "Deforestation" and "supply chain" vocabulary is absent from the indexed ESG corpus, which heavily skews toward energy and emissions language. The retriever falls back to matching the "100%" numeral and returns an AWS energy sentence. The NLI model then predicts SUPPORT (since "100% renewable energy" is a supportive claim), leading to a LOW RISK verdict despite the high-risk nature of the original claim.

This failure mode demonstrates a systematic limitation: **vocabulary mismatch between the claim domain (biodiversity, land use) and the indexed corpus domain (energy, carbon)**. ExxonMobil's extracted content comprises only 49 sentences, limiting coverage of oil & gas supply chain practices. Future work should expand the corpus and employ domain-specific retrieval fine-tuning.

## 8. Discussion and Limitations

### 8.1 The Retrieval Bottleneck

The central finding of this work is that retrieval quality, not NLI accuracy, determines end-to-end greenwashing detection performance. The 73-point gap between component-level macro-F1 (98.18%) and system-level macro-F1 (25.1%) is entirely attributable to retrieval failure: when the correct evidence is provided, the NLI model classifies it almost perfectly. This has clear implications for future work: improving the retriever (through fine-tuning, query expansion, or hybrid sparse-dense retrieval) will yield far greater system-level gains than further NLI fine-tuning.

### 8.2 Oracle vs. Retrieved Evidence Evaluation

A limitation of the current evaluation protocol is that baselines receive oracle evidence while EcoTrace retrieves autonomously. A fairer comparison would evaluate all systems under identical conditions (either all oracle or all retrieval). Under oracle conditions, EcoTrace's NLI component would substantially outperform both baselines. We recommend future work adopt a unified evaluation protocol.

### 8.3 LLM Explanation Hallucination Risk

The Stage 4 explainer uses an LLM to generate justifications. LLMs are prone to producing plausible-sounding but factually incorrect explanations, particularly when retrieved evidence is only tangentially related to the claim. In production deployments, explanations should be treated as aids to human analysts rather than authoritative verdicts, and generation should be conditioned only on verified high-confidence retrieval results.

### 8.4 Label Subjectivity

NLI labelling for greenwashing is inherently ambiguous. The boundary between NEUTRAL (insufficient evidence) and REFUTE (contradictory evidence) is particularly contested: a commitment that has not been achieved could be labelled either way depending on the annotator's reading. Our gold standard uses explicit annotation guidelines (quantitative contradictions → REFUTE; targets without verification → NEUTRAL), but inter-annotator agreement was not formally measured.

### 8.5 PDF Extraction Quality

PyMuPDF extraction from complex multi-column PDF layouts introduces noise in the form of concatenated headers, page numbers, and table cell fragments. Sentences shorter than approximately 20 characters or containing more than 50% numeric content were not filtered, leading to some low-quality index entries. A more robust extraction pipeline using layout-aware PDF parsing (e.g., Adobe's PDF Extract API) would improve corpus quality.

### 8.6 Scope 3 Emissions Coverage

Scope 3 (indirect value chain) emissions, which typically account for 70–90% of a company's total emissions, are rarely discussed in the environmental sentences extracted from our corpus. This means EcoTrace cannot detect greenwashing related to supply chain emissions, a critical area of regulatory scrutiny. Expanding the keyword set to include supply chain, upstream, downstream, and category-specific terms would improve coverage.

---

## 9. Conclusion

We presented EcoTrace, the first end-to-end, explainable pipeline for automated greenwashing detection in ESG corporate disclosures. By combining dense retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, principled risk scoring, and LLM explanation, EcoTrace provides a complete and interpretable greenwashing detection system grounded in specific evidence.

Our principal finding is that the NLI component achieves exceptional accuracy (98.18% macro-F1) when given correct evidence, but end-to-end performance is constrained by retrieval quality. This clearly identifies retrieval as the next frontier for improvement. EcoTrace with retrieved evidence matches TF-IDF baselines with oracle evidence — a noteworthy result given the harder evaluation setting.

Practically, EcoTrace addresses the core failure of existing greenwashing detection tools: it does not merely flag suspicious keywords but identifies specific evidence that contradicts or supports each claim, and explains its reasoning in plain language. These properties make it directly applicable to ESG due diligence workflows, regulatory compliance monitoring, and investigative journalism.

**Future directions** include:
- **Retrieval fine-tuning**: training the bi-encoder on ESG claim–evidence pairs to improve domain-specific retrieval.
- **FAISS indexing**: scaling to thousands of ESG reports with approximate nearest-neighbour search.
- **Multilingual support**: extending the pipeline to non-English ESG disclosures using multilingual sentence encoders.
- **Regulatory integration**: adapting the system to specific reporting frameworks (TCFD, GRI, CSRD) to detect framework-specific non-compliance.
- **Real-time monitoring**: building an API layer for continuous monitoring of corporate press releases and quarterly reports.
