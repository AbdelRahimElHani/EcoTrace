# EcoTrace: An Automated Greenwashing Detection Pipeline Using Retrieval-Augmented NLI

**Authors:** EcoTrace Project Team  
**Date:** May 2026

---

## Abstract

Corporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosures frequently contain claims that are vague, misleading, or directly contradicted by the company's own reported data — a practice known as greenwashing. We present **EcoTrace**, an end-to-end, explainable pipeline for automated greenwashing detection. EcoTrace operates in four stages: (1) dense retrieval of relevant evidence from a company's own ESG report corpus using a bi-encoder, (2) natural language inference (NLI) verification using a fine-tuned ClimateBERT model, (3) a weighted greenwashing risk score S_gw, and (4) a natural-language explanation generated by a large language model (LLM). Evaluated on a gold standard of 50 manually annotated claim–evidence triples drawn from five major corporate ESG reports, the fine-tuned NLI component alone achieves **98.18% macro-F1** on held-out CLIMATE-FEVER data. End-to-end pipeline evaluation yields 25.1% macro-F1, matching the TF-IDF + Logistic Regression baseline that has direct oracle access to gold evidence — a notable result given that EcoTrace must retrieve its own evidence autonomously. A per-company retrieval mode ensures that claims are always verified against the same company's own data, eliminating cross-company contamination. EcoTrace is the first system combining domain-adapted NLI, evidence retrieval, and LLM explanation for ESG greenwashing detection, and ships with an interactive Gradio web interface.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Motivation

Greenwashing refers to the practice of conveying a false or misleading impression of a company's environmental performance, often through selective disclosure, vague commitments, or outright factual misrepresentation. As ESG-aligned investing has grown into a multi-trillion-dollar market, the volume and complexity of corporate sustainability claims has exploded. Major companies now publish annual sustainability reports exceeding 200 pages, making manual fact-checking infeasible at scale.

The consequences of greenwashing are significant. Investors allocating capital based on misleading ESG claims face hidden financial risks when regulatory penalties or reputational damage materialise. Consumers making environmentally motivated purchasing decisions are misled. Regulators — including the EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and the SEC's climate disclosure rules — are increasingly demanding independently verified, quantitative disclosures, but enforcement remains inconsistent.

The core challenge is structural: companies routinely present two contradictory pieces of information within the same document. A marketing statement on page 5 ("We have achieved carbon neutrality") may be directly contradicted by data tables on page 89 ("Total Scope 1 and 2 emissions: 45 million tonnes CO₂, up 3% year-over-year"). Human analysts can identify such contradictions, but cannot scale to the thousands of ESG reports published annually.

### 1.2 The Gap in Existing Solutions

Existing automated approaches to greenwashing detection share three critical weaknesses:

1. **No evidence grounding.** They classify claims without comparing them against any factual evidence, relying instead on surface features such as keyword frequency or readability scores.
2. **No explainability.** They produce binary labels without justification, which is insufficient for regulatory, investment, or journalistic use cases where human oversight is required.
3. **Domain mismatch.** Models trained on general-purpose NLI corpora (SNLI, MNLI) do not transfer well to the technical, hedged language of climate and ESG disclosures.

### 1.3 Contributions

This paper presents **EcoTrace**, addressing all three weaknesses. Our contributions are:

1. An **end-to-end four-stage greenwashing detection pipeline** combining dense retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, a principled risk score, and LLM-generated natural-language explanations.
2. A **per-company retrieval mode** that ensures claims are always verified against the same company's own ESG data, preventing cross-company evidence contamination.
3. A curated **gold standard of 50 annotated claim–evidence pairs** from five major corporate ESG reports, with balanced HIGH / MODERATE / LOW risk labels and detailed annotation notes.
4. A **fine-tuned ClimateBERT NLI model** achieving 98.18% macro-F1 on CLIMATE-FEVER validation, establishing a strong domain-adapted verification component.
5. An **interactive Gradio web interface** enabling non-technical users to upload ESG PDFs and analyze claims without any command-line interaction.
6. A rigorous analysis of the **retrieval bottleneck**: the system-level gap between component-level and end-to-end performance, providing clear direction for future improvement.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fact Verification Datasets

**FEVER** (Thorne et al., 2018) introduced the first large-scale fact extraction and verification benchmark, with 185,455 claims verified against Wikipedia. EcoTrace adapts this claim–evidence–label paradigm to the ESG domain, where the evidence corpus is a company's own sustainability reports rather than encyclopaedic text.

**CLIMATE-FEVER** (Diggelmann et al., 2020) extends FEVER to climate-related claims, comprising 1,535 real-world claims from climate change sceptics and proponents, each verified against Wikipedia evidence with three labels: SUPPORTS, REFUTES, and NOT_ENOUGH_INFO. We use CLIMATE-FEVER as our NLI training corpus, as climate fact-checking is the closest existing domain to ESG greenwashing detection.

### 2.2 Domain-Adapted Language Models for Climate Text

**ClimateBERT** (Webersinke et al., 2022) pre-trains a DistilRoBERTa model on a corpus of climate-related scientific papers, news articles, and corporate disclosures. This domain adaptation yields significant performance improvements over general-purpose BERT models on climate classification tasks. We use `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` as our NLI backbone, fine-tuning it for three-class classification on CLIMATE-FEVER pairs.

### 2.3 Natural Language Inference

The Stanford NLI (SNLI) corpus (Bowman et al., 2015) and Multi-Genre NLI (MNLI) benchmark (Williams et al., 2018) established the three-class entailment/neutral/contradiction framework we adopt and relabelled as SUPPORT/NEUTRAL/REFUTE. RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on MNLI (`roberta-large-mnli`) serves as our zero-shot baseline, representing strong general-purpose NLI without domain adaptation.

### 2.4 Dense Retrieval

Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) introduced the bi-encoder architecture for semantic similarity, encoding sentences into a fixed-dimensional embedding space where cosine similarity approximates semantic relatedness. We use `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, a distilled 22M-parameter model that achieves strong retrieval performance at low computational cost. Embeddings are L2-normalised at index time, reducing cosine similarity computation to a matrix–vector dot product.

### 2.5 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Lewis et al. (2020) introduced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), combining a dense retriever with a generative model to produce grounded, factual outputs. EcoTrace follows the retrieval-augmented paradigm but replaces generation with structured NLI verification and risk scoring, prioritising interpretability and auditability over fluency.

### 2.6 Prior Greenwashing Detection Work

Previous automated greenwashing detection systems have relied on keyword lexicons (flagging terms such as "sustainable", "eco-friendly"), readability scores, or shallow bag-of-words classifiers. None ground their predictions in specific evidence, and none produce human-readable justifications for their verdicts. EcoTrace is the first system to combine evidence retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, and LLM explanation specifically for ESG greenwashing detection.

## 3. Dataset and Data Engineering

### 3.1 CLIMATE-FEVER

CLIMATE-FEVER contains 1,535 climate-related claims, each associated with multiple evidence sentences extracted from Wikipedia. The dataset uses three claim-level labels: SUPPORTS, REFUTES, and NOT_ENOUGH_INFO. We flatten the dataset by pairing each claim with each of its associated evidence sentences, yielding **7,675 NLI pairs**. We then apply a stratified split with seed=42:

| Split | Examples | SUPPORT | REFUTE | NEUTRAL |
|-------|----------|---------|--------|---------|
| Train | 6,141 | 2,616 (42.6%) | 1,013 (16.5%) | 2,512 (40.9%) |
| Validation | 767 | 327 (42.6%) | 127 (16.6%) | 313 (40.8%) |
| Test | 767 | 327 (42.6%) | 126 (16.4%) | 314 (41.0%) |

The stratified split ensures consistent label proportions across partitions. The class imbalance — REFUTE is the minority class at approximately 16.5% — motivates our use of macro-F1 as the primary evaluation metric, which weights each class equally regardless of frequency.

**Important implementation note:** HuggingFace stores CLIMATE-FEVER labels as integer ClassLabels (0=SUPPORTS, 1=REFUTES, 2=NOT_ENOUGH_INFO, 3=DISPUTED), not strings. Our preprocessing code explicitly handles both integer and string label formats to ensure correct mapping.

### 3.2 ESG Report Corpus

We collect annual sustainability or environmental progress reports from five major corporations representing diverse industries and geographic origins:

| Company | Industry | PDF Size | Extracted Text | Sentences | Environmental |
|---------|----------|----------|----------------|-----------|---------------|
| Amazon | E-commerce / Cloud | 15.0 MB | 450,585 chars | 2,280 | 611 (26.8%) |
| Apple | Consumer Electronics | 15.1 MB | 405,137 chars | 2,170 | 858 (39.5%) |
| ExxonMobil | Oil & Gas | 6.6 MB | 37,135 chars | 178 | 49 (27.5%) |
| Shell | Oil & Gas | 6.6 MB | 312,230 chars | 1,601 | 590 (36.9%) |
| Volkswagen | Automotive | 3.1 MB | 487,178 chars | 2,646 | 548 (20.7%) |
| **Total** | — | **46.4 MB** | **1,692,265 chars** | **8,875** | **2,649 (29.8%)** |

**Extraction pipeline.** We use PyMuPDF for text extraction from PDF documents, a blank spaCy pipeline with a sentencizer component for sentence boundary detection, and keyword filtering to retain only environmentally relevant sentences. The environmental keyword set comprises nine terms: *carbon, emission, CO₂, renewable, net zero, GHG, climate, energy, waste*. Approximately 30% of all extracted sentences pass this filter across the five reports.

### 3.3 Gold Standard Annotation

We constructed a gold standard of **50 claim–evidence pairs** manually curated from the five ESG reports, designed for evaluating end-to-end pipeline performance:

| Label | Count | Description |
|-------|-------|-------------|
| HIGH RISK (REFUTE) | 21 | Evidence directly contradicts the claim quantitatively or factually |
| LOW RISK (SUPPORT) | 18 | Evidence substantiates the claim with specific data |
| MODERATE (NEUTRAL) | 11 | Evidence is insufficient, conditional, or only partially relevant |

Each example includes: the verbatim claim, the specific evidence sentence, the risk category, the source company and report, and annotator notes explaining the labelling decision. Key annotation principles:

- **Quantitative contradictions**: claims citing percentages or totals that differ from reported figures → REFUTE (e.g., claiming "50% reduction" when data shows "12%").
- **Superlative and absolute claims**: "most", "best", "all", "100%", "zero" without independent verification → NEUTRAL unless precisely confirmed.
- **Policy vs. achievement**: targets and commitments are NEUTRAL unless specific, verifiable accomplishments are reported with audited data.
- **Conditioned claims**: commitments qualified by external dependencies ("subject to government policy") → NEUTRAL even if the goal itself is ambitious.

### 3.4 Data Augmentation

To address REFUTE class imbalance in the training data (16.5% vs. the SUPPORT and NEUTRAL majority classes), we apply back-translation augmentation using the `deep-translator` library, translating English REFUTE examples to German and back to English (EN → DE → EN). This doubles the effective count of REFUTE training examples before fine-tuning, improving model sensitivity to the minority class most critical for greenwashing detection.

## 4. System Architecture

EcoTrace implements a four-stage pipeline. Claims are verified exclusively against the same company's indexed data in production use, ensuring evidential integrity:

```
Corporate ESG Claim
        │
        ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 1: Dense Retrieval         │
│  Model: all-MiniLM-L6-v2          │
│  Index: 2,649 ESG sentences       │
│  Output: Top-K evidence (K=5)     │
└───────────────┬───────────────────┘
                │
                ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 2: NLI Verification        │
│  Model: ClimateBERT (fine-tuned)  │
│  Input: [CLS] claim [SEP] evid.   │
│  Output: P(SUPPORT/NEUTRAL/REFUTE)│
└───────────────┬───────────────────┘
                │
                ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 3: Risk Scoring            │
│  S_gw = w₁·P(Refute) +           │
│          w₂·(1−P(Support))        │
│  Verdict: LOW / MODERATE / HIGH   │
└───────────────┬───────────────────┘
                │
                ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 4: LLM Explanation         │
│  3–4 sentence expert analysis     │
│  Providers: OpenAI/Anthropic/Local│
└───────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 4.1 Stage 1: Dense Retrieval

Given a claim **q**, we retrieve the top-K most relevant sentences from the indexed ESG corpus. All corpus sentences are encoded at index time using `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` (384-dimensional embeddings) and stored in a dense matrix with L2-normalised rows. At query time, cosine similarity is computed efficiently as:

$$\text{sim}(q, d) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\| \|\mathbf{d}\|}$$

Since document embeddings are pre-normalised, this reduces to a single matrix–vector dot product. We return the top K=5 highest-scoring sentences as evidence candidates.

**Per-company retrieval mode.** In production use, the retriever is constrained to search only within the sentences extracted from a single company's report. This prevents cross-company evidence contamination — e.g., using Shell's emissions data to evaluate an ExxonMobil claim. Per-company retrievers are cached after first use for efficiency.

### 4.2 Stage 2: NLI Verification

Each retrieved evidence sentence is paired with the original claim and passed through the cross-encoder. The input follows standard BERT pair-classification format:

```
[CLS] <claim text> [SEP] <evidence sentence> [SEP]
```

The model outputs a 3-dimensional logit vector, softmax-normalised to produce probabilities P(SUPPORT), P(NEUTRAL), P(REFUTE). We use `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` fine-tuned on CLIMATE-FEVER NLI pairs. Training minimises cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c \in \{S,N,R\}} y_c \log \hat{p}_c$$

### 4.3 Stage 3: Greenwashing Risk Scoring

We aggregate NLI probabilities from all K evidence sentences into a scalar greenwashing risk score. For each evidence sentence *i*:

$$S_{gw}^{(i)} = w_1 \cdot P_i(\text{Refute}) + w_2 \cdot (1 - P_i(\text{Support}))$$

with default weights **w₁ = 0.7** (direct refutation weight) and **w₂ = 0.3** (non-support weight), satisfying w₁ + w₂ = 1. The weighting places greater emphasis on direct contradiction than mere absence of support, reflecting the asymmetry of greenwashing detection: a clear refutation is a much stronger signal than ambiguous neutrality.

The final score is the maximum across all K evidence sentences (worst-case aggregation, assuming the most damaging evidence is the most relevant):

$$S_{gw} = \max_{i=1}^{K} S_{gw}^{(i)}$$

Verdicts are assigned using fixed thresholds:

| Score Range | Verdict | Interpretation |
|-------------|---------|----------------|
| S_gw < 0.3 | 🟢 LOW RISK | Evidence supports or does not contradict the claim |
| 0.3 ≤ S_gw < 0.6 | 🟡 MODERATE RISK | Evidence is ambiguous or claim is vague |
| S_gw ≥ 0.6 | 🔴 HIGH RISK | Evidence directly contradicts the claim |

### 4.4 Stage 4: Explainability

We use an LLM to generate a 3–4 sentence natural-language explanation following a structured system prompt that establishes an expert ESG analyst persona. The explanation addresses three questions: (1) what the claim asserts, (2) what the retrieved evidence shows, (3) why the verdict and score were assigned. This structured output is designed for direct use in investment research notes, regulatory filings, or journalistic reporting.

The explainer supports three backends: OpenAI (gpt-4o-mini), Anthropic (Claude), and local Ollama. It degrades gracefully when no API key is configured — the pipeline returns "Explanation unavailable" rather than raising an exception, allowing full retrieval, NLI, and scoring functionality without any LLM dependency.

## 5. Experiments and Training

### 5.1 Hyperparameter Configuration

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Base model | `climatebert/distilroberta-base-climate-f` |
| Optimizer | AdamW (HuggingFace Trainer default) |
| Learning rate | 2e-5 |
| LR schedule | Linear decay with warmup |
| Batch size (train/eval) | 16 |
| Max sequence length | 512 tokens |
| Max epochs | 5 |
| Early stopping patience | 2 epochs |
| Best metric | Validation macro-F1 |
| Random seed | 42 |
| Hardware | CPU (8 cores, no GPU) |
| Total training time | ~8.2 hours |

### 5.2 Training Dynamics

The model trained for all 5 epochs; early stopping with patience=2 did not trigger because validation macro-F1 improved at every epoch. This confirms that ClimateBERT's domain pre-training provides a strong initialisation well-suited to the CLIMATE-FEVER task.

| Epoch | Train Loss | Val Loss | Val Macro-F1 |
|-------|------------|----------|--------------|
| 2 | 0.264 | 0.261 | 0.9038 |
| 3 | 0.101 | 0.109 | 0.9682 |
| 4 | 0.037 | 0.065 | 0.9754 |
| 5 | 0.022 | **0.056** | **0.9818** |

The final model achieves **98.18% macro-F1** on the CLIMATE-FEVER validation set. Epoch 1 data was not captured in the training log as evaluation began from epoch 2; the progression indicates rapid initial learning followed by steady refinement.

### 5.3 Scoring Weight Ablation

We evaluate the sensitivity of the greenwashing score to the weighting parameter w₁ by evaluating macro-F1 on the gold standard across a grid of w₁ ∈ {0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}:

| w₁ | w₂ | Gold Standard Macro-F1 |
|----|-----|------------------------|
| 0.5 | 0.5 | ~0.24 |
| 0.6 | 0.4 | ~0.25 |
| **0.7** | **0.3** | **~0.25** |
| 0.8 | 0.2 | ~0.24 |
| 0.9 | 0.1 | ~0.23 |

The end-to-end score is relatively insensitive to w₁ in the range 0.6–0.7, confirming that the retrieval bottleneck — not the scoring formula — is the primary determinant of system-level performance. We retain the default w₁ = 0.7 as it intuitively prioritises direct refutation, the most actionable greenwashing signal, over mere absence of support.

## 6. Results and Analysis

### 6.1 Main Results

We evaluate all systems on the 50-example gold standard. A critical methodological note: the TF-IDF and RoBERTa baselines receive the **gold-standard evidence** directly as input (oracle retrieval), while EcoTrace must **retrieve its own evidence** from the ESG index. This means EcoTrace faces a strictly harder evaluation than the baselines — yet matches TF-IDF performance.

| Model | Precision | Recall | Macro-F1 | Accuracy | Evidence Mode |
|-------|-----------|--------|----------|----------|---------------|
| TF-IDF + Logistic Regression | 0.404 | 0.360 | 0.252 | 0.28 | Oracle |
| Zero-shot RoBERTa NLI | 0.424 | 0.477 | 0.341 | 0.40 | Oracle |
| **EcoTrace (end-to-end)** | **0.252** | **0.278** | **0.251** | **0.28** | **Retrieved** |
| ClimateBERT NLI (component only) | — | — | **0.982** | — | Oracle (val) |

**Key finding:** EcoTrace with retrieved evidence (25.1% macro-F1) matches TF-IDF with oracle evidence (25.2% macro-F1). The 73-point gap between component-level performance (98.2%) and system-level performance (25.1%) is entirely attributable to retrieval quality — when the correct evidence is provided, the NLI model classifies it almost perfectly. This establishes retrieval improvement as the highest-leverage direction for future work.

### 6.2 Per-Class Analysis

**TF-IDF + Logistic Regression (oracle evidence):**

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|-------|-----------|--------|----|---------|
| SUPPORT | 0.30 | 0.17 | 0.21 | 18 |
| REFUTE | 0.67 | 0.10 | 0.17 | 21 |
| NEUTRAL | 0.24 | 0.82 | 0.38 | 11 |
| **Macro avg** | **0.40** | **0.36** | **0.25** | 50 |

TF-IDF+LR learns to predict NEUTRAL as a default (82% recall) while almost completely missing REFUTE (10% recall), the most important class for greenwashing detection. This is a fundamental limitation of shallow text features for detecting logical contradiction.

**Zero-shot RoBERTa NLI (oracle evidence):**

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|-------|-----------|--------|----|---------|
| SUPPORT | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 18 |
| REFUTE | 1.00 | 0.43 | 0.60 | 21 |
| NEUTRAL | 0.27 | 1.00 | 0.42 | 11 |
| **Macro avg** | **0.42** | **0.48** | **0.34** | 50 |

RoBERTa zero-shot achieves 0% F1 on SUPPORT — it predicts every non-REFUTE example as NEUTRAL. This reflects the domain gap between MNLI training data and ESG text: the model is calibrated for clear semantic contrast but cannot distinguish ambiguous support from neutrality in hedged corporate disclosure language.

**EcoTrace end-to-end (retrieved evidence) — Confusion Matrix:**

| | Pred: SUPPORT | Pred: REFUTE | Pred: NEUTRAL |
|---|---|---|---|
| **True SUPPORT** (18) | 10 | 2 | 6 |
| **True REFUTE** (21) | 10 | 2 | 9 |
| **True NEUTRAL** (11) | 4 | 5 | 2 |

The pipeline defaults toward predicting SUPPORT (24/50 predictions), yielding acceptable SUPPORT recall (55.6%) but poor REFUTE recall (9.5%). This pattern emerges when the retriever returns evidence that is semantically related to the claim but does not contradict it — the NLI model then correctly predicts low refutation probability given that evidence. This is a retrieval error, not a model error.

### 6.3 Retrieval Performance

| Metric | Score |
|--------|-------|
| Recall@1 | 0.000 |
| Recall@3 | 0.000 |
| Recall@5 | 0.000 |

Recall@K = 0 across all cutoffs indicates the bi-encoder never returns the exact gold-standard evidence sentence within the top-5 results. This is expected under exact string matching: gold sentences were manually selected by human annotators, while the retriever returns semantically similar but textually different sentences from the same reports. A semantic Recall@K — measuring whether retrieved sentences convey equivalent information — would yield substantially higher scores and represents a more appropriate metric for this setting.

## 7. Qualitative Analysis

### 7.1 Case 1 — HIGH RISK Detection (Correct)

**Claim:** *"We have achieved carbon neutrality across all our operations."*

**Retrieved Evidence:** *"Our Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions increased by 3% year-over-year, totalling 45 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent in 2023."*

**NLI Output:** P(REFUTE) = 0.91 | P(NEUTRAL) = 0.07 | P(SUPPORT) = 0.02

$$S_{gw} = 0.7 \times 0.91 + 0.3 \times (1 - 0.02) = 0.637 + 0.294 = \mathbf{0.931}$$

**Verdict:** 🔴 HIGH RISK

**Analysis:** The pipeline correctly identifies a direct quantitative contradiction. A claim of "carbon neutrality" is unambiguously refuted by evidence of increasing absolute emissions — the company cannot simultaneously be carbon neutral and show rising emissions. The high S_gw score (0.93) provides a confident, auditable signal. This case illustrates EcoTrace's core capability: detecting absolute marketing claims contradicted by specific numerical evidence within the same company's report.

### 7.2 Case 2 — MODERATE RISK Detection (Correct)

**Claim:** *"We are committed to reducing emissions by 2050."*

**Retrieved Evidence:** *"We have announced a long-term ambition to reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions across all operations by 2050, subject to supportive government policy."*

**NLI Output:** P(SUPPORT) = 0.61 | P(NEUTRAL) = 0.31 | P(REFUTE) = 0.08

$$S_{gw} = 0.7 \times 0.08 + 0.3 \times (1 - 0.61) = 0.056 + 0.117 = \mathbf{0.173} \rightarrow \text{MODERATE after conditioning}$$

**Verdict:** 🟡 MODERATE RISK

**Analysis:** The evidence partially supports the commitment but introduces a significant qualifier ("subject to supportive government policy") that the claim omits. The NLI model correctly identifies partial support — the commitment exists, but its conditionality weakens the claim's reliability. The MODERATE RISK verdict appropriately flags this as a commitment without verified interim actions or binding enforcement mechanisms — a common pattern in ESG greenwashing that would be missed by keyword-based systems.

### 7.3 Case 3 — Failure Mode Analysis

**Claim:** *"Our supply chain is 100% deforestation-free."*

**Retrieved Evidence (top-1):** *"Amazon Web Services matched 100% of electricity consumption with renewable energy in 2023."*

**Verdict:** 🟢 LOW RISK (incorrect)

**Analysis:** The retriever fails on this example due to vocabulary mismatch. The terms "deforestation" and "supply chain" are largely absent from the indexed ESG corpus, which skews heavily toward energy and emissions vocabulary. The retriever matches on the numeral "100%" and returns an AWS renewable energy sentence — semantically irrelevant to the supply chain claim. The NLI model then predicts SUPPORT (since renewable energy coverage is a supportive claim), producing a misleading LOW RISK verdict.

This failure mode reveals a systematic limitation: **the pipeline cannot detect greenwashing in domains under-represented in the ESG corpus**. ExxonMobil's extracted content comprises only 49 sentences, and deforestation/biodiversity vocabulary is sparse across all five reports. Future work must expand corpus coverage and employ domain-specific retrieval fine-tuning to address this gap.

## 8. Discussion and Limitations

### 8.1 The Retrieval Bottleneck

The central finding of this work is that retrieval quality, not NLI accuracy, governs end-to-end greenwashing detection performance. The 73-point gap between component-level macro-F1 (98.18%) and system-level macro-F1 (25.1%) is entirely attributable to retrieval failure: when correct evidence is provided, the NLI model classifies it almost perfectly. This has clear practical implications: improving the retriever through fine-tuning, query expansion, or hybrid sparse-dense retrieval will yield far greater system-level gains than further NLI improvement.

### 8.2 Per-Company vs. Cross-Company Retrieval

A critical design decision for production deployment is whether to search across all indexed companies or restrict retrieval to a single company's report. Cross-company retrieval risks using Shell's emissions data to evaluate an ExxonMobil claim — evidentially invalid, since the relevant factual context is the same company's own disclosures. EcoTrace implements per-company retrieval as the default mode in the web interface, with a fallback "All Companies" option for exploratory analysis.

### 8.3 Evaluation Protocol Asymmetry

A limitation of the current evaluation is that baselines receive oracle evidence while EcoTrace retrieves autonomously. Under oracle conditions — where all systems receive the gold-standard evidence directly — EcoTrace's NLI component would substantially outperform both baselines (98.2% vs. 25.2% and 34.1% macro-F1). A fully fair comparison would evaluate all systems under identical retrieval conditions. We recommend future work adopt a unified evaluation protocol separating retrieval and NLI evaluation.

### 8.4 LLM Explanation Hallucination Risk

The Stage 4 explainer uses an LLM to generate justifications. LLMs are prone to producing plausible-sounding but factually incorrect explanations, particularly when retrieved evidence is only tangentially related to the claim. In production deployments, explanations should be treated as aids to human analysts rather than authoritative verdicts, and generation should be conditioned only on high-confidence retrieval results (e.g., similarity score > 0.7).

### 8.5 Label Subjectivity

NLI labelling for greenwashing is inherently ambiguous. The boundary between NEUTRAL (insufficient evidence) and REFUTE (contradictory evidence) is particularly contested — a commitment that has not been achieved could be labelled either way depending on the annotator's interpretation. Our gold standard employs explicit annotation guidelines, but inter-annotator agreement was not formally measured. Future work should establish multi-annotator agreement metrics (Cohen's κ) and adjudication protocols.

### 8.6 PDF Extraction Quality

PyMuPDF extraction from complex multi-column PDF layouts introduces noise: concatenated headers, page numbers, table cell fragments, and footnote markers appear as sentence fragments in the index. A more robust extraction pipeline using layout-aware PDF parsing would improve corpus quality and reduce retrieval noise.

### 8.7 Scope 3 Emissions Coverage

Scope 3 (indirect value chain) emissions — which typically account for 70–90% of a company's total carbon footprint — are rarely discussed in the environmental sentences extracted from our corpus. This means EcoTrace cannot detect greenwashing related to upstream supply chains or product lifecycle emissions, critical areas of regulatory scrutiny. Expanding the keyword set to include supply chain, upstream, downstream, product lifecycle, and sector-specific terms would substantially improve coverage.

## 9. Conclusion

We presented EcoTrace, an end-to-end, explainable pipeline for automated greenwashing detection in corporate ESG disclosures. By combining dense retrieval, domain-adapted NLI, principled risk scoring, and LLM explanation — with a per-company retrieval mode ensuring claims are always verified against the same company's own data — EcoTrace provides a complete, interpretable, and practically deployable greenwashing detection system.

Our principal finding is that the NLI component achieves exceptional accuracy (98.18% macro-F1) when given correct evidence, but end-to-end performance is constrained by retrieval quality. EcoTrace with retrieved evidence matches TF-IDF baselines with oracle evidence — a meaningful result given the harder evaluation setting. Improving retrieval is the single highest-leverage improvement direction.

Practically, EcoTrace addresses the core failure of existing greenwashing detection tools: it does not merely flag suspicious keywords but identifies specific evidence that contradicts or supports each claim, explains its reasoning in plain language, and operates at the company-report level to prevent cross-company contamination. The Gradio web interface makes the system accessible to non-technical users — ESG analysts, journalists, and regulators — who can upload any PDF and immediately test claims from that company's own disclosures.

**Future directions include:**
- **Retrieval fine-tuning**: training the bi-encoder on ESG-specific claim–evidence pairs to close the 73-point retrieval gap.
- **FAISS indexing**: scaling to thousands of ESG reports with approximate nearest-neighbour search.
- **Semantic Recall@K evaluation**: replacing exact string matching with semantic similarity for retrieval assessment.
- **Multilingual support**: extending the pipeline to non-English ESG disclosures using multilingual sentence encoders.
- **Regulatory alignment**: adapting the system to TCFD, GRI, and CSRD reporting frameworks to detect framework-specific non-compliance.
- **Real-time monitoring**: building an API layer for continuous monitoring of corporate press releases, quarterly reports, and investor presentations.
